=== Part 3: why the committed row survives (the write-ahead log) ===
Changing a table page in memory is not durable: the page may be written to disk much later, and a
power cut or crash in between would lose the change. PostgreSQL therefore follows the write-ahead
rule: before any data page change may reach disk, a WAL record describing the change (the "redo"
information) must be written to the WAL, an append-only log on disk.

When a transaction commits, PostgreSQL appends a COMMIT record to the WAL and flushes the WAL to
disk (fsync) BEFORE it tells the client the commit succeeded. That is what synchronous_commit = on
and fsync = on above mean: once COMMIT returns, the record is on stable storage, even though the
modified data pages may still be only in shared memory. The sequential WAL write is also cheap
compared with writing many scattered data pages, which is why the data pages can be written lazily
by the background writer and by checkpoints. The LSN (log sequence number) in Part 1 is the byte
position in the WAL; it advanced for the one committed insert (the figure includes any other sessions writing at the same time, since the WAL is shared), and the
flushed LSN caught up with the current LSN at COMMIT.

After a crash, the server starts in recovery. It reads pg_control to find the last checkpoint, then
replays (redoes) every WAL record from that checkpoint's redo point forward, re-applying changes
whose data pages never reached disk. Transactions whose COMMIT record is in the WAL are restored;
transactions without one are treated as aborted. A checkpoint is the point at which all dirty pages
up to a known LSN have been flushed, so recovery never needs to look further back than that.
full_page_writes = on protects against torn pages: the first change to a page after a checkpoint
logs the whole page image, so recovery can repair a half-written page. wal_level = replica is the
default level, enough for crash recovery and for physical replication or backups.
